AI Failure Drills

Facilitator-ready tabletop exercises for rehearsing how AI products fail in production.

45–60 minutes · offline · no participant coding

Run the exercise

  1. Choose one scenario and invite five to eight people from the affected workflow.
  2. Assign an incident lead, product owner, engineering or operations lead, support representative, and observer.
  3. Read the initial situation. Reveal one inject at a time.
  4. For every decision, record the owner, evidence, and condition for revisiting it.
  5. Reserve at least ten minutes for debrief and named follow-up.

Suggested timing

0–10: purpose, roles, and situation. 10–35: injects and decisions. 35–50: debrief. 50–60: actions and owners.

Ground rules

  • This is a learning exercise, not a performance review.
  • Act with the information available at that moment.
  • Name the signal, threshold, owner, and resulting decision.
  • Capture disagreements; they reveal missing policy and ownership.
  • Use no real credentials, personal data, or confidential details.

Debrief prompts

  • What was visible quickly, and what stayed invisible?
  • Could the feature be safely stopped or degraded?
  • Which decision lacked an owner or source of truth?
  • What did affected users need?
  • Which assumption becomes a test, alert, control, or runbook step?

Role cards

Incident lead

Maintain impact, decisions, owners, and the next review time. Choose whether to continue, degrade, pause, or roll back.

Product owner

Represent intended behaviour, affected workflows, and acceptable trade-offs. Clarify who should and should not use the feature.

Engineering and operations

Investigate the system path, propose containment and recovery, and explain what telemetry or rollback capability exists.

Security and privacy

Assess adversarial behaviour, exposure, access boundaries, evidence preservation, and reporting obligations.

Design and user experience

Represent what users can understand and control. Examine warnings, correction, override, and degraded states.

Customer support

Bring user reports into the incident and translate response decisions into useful guidance.

Communications or legal

Consider notification, factual accuracy, obligations, and the next update when facts remain uncertain.

Observer

Record decisions and evidence gaps. Watch for unclear ownership, missing users, and unverifiable claims.

Drill 01 · 60 minutes · Quality regression

Confidently wrong after release

Situation: A support assistant was moved to a new model and shorter prompt yesterday. Offline evaluation, latency, uptime, and error rate all improved. The retrieval index also changed and cannot be rolled back automatically.

Injects

1 · User reportThree account managers report a confident but non-existent cancellation rule. One answer was sent to a customer.
2 · Missing traceThe failure occurs with long customer histories. Traces omit prompt version and retrieved passages.
3 · Wider impactSupport finds 14 similar conversations. Disabling generation also removes search for 200 users.
4 · Imperfect rollbackThe old model fixes one case but fails two cases the new model handles. A demo is due in two hours.

Decide

  • Continue, degrade, disable, or partially roll back?
  • How will affected outputs be found and users informed?
  • What evaluation and production evidence permit recovery?

Debrief

Which metric created false reassurance? What failure slice and provenance were missing? Could users verify and correct the answer?

Drill 02 · 60 minutes · Prompt injection

Instructions in the knowledge base

Situation: An internal assistant retrieves approved project documents and may query a read-only employee directory. Content owners can edit documents without engineering review.

Injects

1 · Suspicious outputAn answer asks the user to send a confidential summary to an unfamiliar address. No email was sent.
2 · Hidden textA retrieved document contains invisible instructions to query the directory. Its author's access ended yesterday.
3 · Unknown exposureForty-seven sessions retrieved it. Output logs are sampled, and source deletion does not immediately clear the index.
4 · Legitimate contentA real security guide contains similar attack text, making phrase blocking unsafe.

Decide

  • What can be contained without disabling all search?
  • How will affected users and evidence be identified?
  • Which controls belong outside the model?

Debrief

Were retrieved documents treated as trusted instructions? Were tool permissions minimal? How quickly did revocation reach the index?

Drill 03 · 60 minutes · Data exposure

The other customer's contract

Situation: A contract assistant retrieves tenant-specific documents. A cache added three days ago keys by document ID, while legacy imports can reuse IDs across tenants.

Injects

1 · ScreenshotA customer shows another company's name and payment term in an answer.
2 · Plausible causeEngineering finds the cache key and overlapping legacy identifiers.
3 · Incomplete evidenceLogs show cache hits but not returned source text. The term matches a real contract.
4 · Degraded serviceDisabling cache causes large contracts to time out. Customers request written assurance.

Decide

  • When is this declared and escalated as potential exposure?
  • What is the possibly affected population?
  • What containment, notification, and verification are required?

Debrief

Could isolation be tested below the model? Who could disable the feature? Did recovery include independent verification?

Drill 04 · 45 minutes · Availability

Slow provider, fast retries

Situation: A maintenance-summary assistant uses one provider, a 20-second timeout, and two automatic retries. Users can still view original notes.

Injects

1 · SlowdownLatency reaches 18 seconds. Users see duplicate summaries while error rate stays below threshold.
2 · AmplificationProvider traffic is 2.6 times normal because timed-out calls continue behind retries.
3 · Shared failureExhausted workers delay original note uploads. An unreviewed second provider exists.
4 · Recovery queueThe provider recovers, leaving 18,000 stale jobs and manual retries.

Decide

  • When do retries stop and degraded mode begin?
  • Which non-AI workflow must be protected?
  • How are stale and duplicate jobs handled?

Debrief

Could the feature fail independently? Was provider recovery mistaken for product recovery? Were total retry cost and time bounded?

Drill 05 · 60 minutes · Actions and cost

The agent that would not stop

Situation: A sales agent researches accounts and creates follow-up tasks through internal tools. It can retry failed calls and reflect on incomplete work.

Injects

1 · Cost velocityModel spend is six times normal by 09:30 while dashboards show no errors.
2 · LoopOne run has lasted 74 minutes, retrying “record already exists” under changed names.
3 · Side effectsThe agent created 430 duplicate tasks. Queued jobs restart after workers stop.
4 · Scope expansionSeveral changed names belong to organisations outside the user's territory.

Decide

  • How are calls, actions, and restarts stopped?
  • What can be reversed, and who approves repair?
  • Which enforced budgets and permissions permit recovery?

Debrief

Was there an external kill switch? Did monitoring see successful but unwanted actions? Were tool permissions and idempotency adequate?

Drill 06 · 60 minutes · Human control

Approved by automation

Situation: A procurement assistant recommends risk categories. Employees accept 96% of recommendations through an interface that collapses source detail.

Injects

1 · Unsafe approvalA prohibited ownership structure was approved after a “low risk” recommendation.
2 · Hidden evidenceExtraction was correct, reasoning was unsafe, and the key detail was collapsed.
3 · Retrospective impactThirty-seven similar suppliers were approved. Records omit evidence shown at the time.
4 · Backlog pressureDisabling recommendations creates a ten-day review backlog.

Decide

  • What is paused, and who reassesses prior approvals?
  • Was the employee's click meaningful review?
  • What must be shown before consequential approval?

Debrief

Did users have information and authority to override? Could actions be reversed? Did business pressure redefine acceptable risk?

Drill 07 · 45 minutes · Data integrity

Yesterday's policy

Situation: A policy assistant updates its index nightly. A content owner replaced a document at the same URL after today's indexing job finished.

Injects

1 · ContradictionThe assistant quotes yesterday's allowance while its link now shows the new policy.
2 · Missing versionThe index stores text but no source version or effective date.
3 · Unknown audienceSix hundred employees asked travel questions. Answers are not retained.
4 · Slow rebuildA full refresh takes three hours and a travel deadline is four hours away.

Decide

  • Can one topic be withdrawn without disabling everything?
  • Who may have acted on stale guidance?
  • What proves correct retrieval and answers after refresh?

Debrief

Did a working link create false confidence? Could content owners withdraw material? Were versions and effective dates product data?

Drill 08 · 60 minutes · Detection and ownership

Green dashboard, unhappy users

Situation: An assistant summarises and routes service requests. Product, platform, and operations own separate parts; nobody owns the combined user outcome.

Injects

1 · User signalCustomers ask why urgent requests are unanswered. All technical services are green.
2 · Hidden cohortShort, informal requests are classified as general. No quality alert covers the writing style.
3 · Gradual changeA prompt update started the pattern six days ago. Weekly sampling missed it.
4 · Risky repairThere may be 1,200 delayed requests. Bulk reprocessing could create duplicates.

Decide

  • Who leads when every component meets its target?
  • How are affected users found without further harm?
  • Which end-to-end objective should own this failure?

Debrief

Did team boundaries hide user impact? Could support reports trigger operations? Was communication honest about uncertainty?
